01 · Standard operating procedures

Clear steps for every task

Every business, service and professional practice has work that repeats: onboarding a client, booking appointments, preparing a proposal, handling payments, sending invoices, closing out a project, etc.  An SOP writes down the agreed way to do it. It gets done the same way no matter who does it.

What this does for you

It works the same for a shop, a clinic, a law or accounting office, an agency, a consultant or an executive’s office. Without an SOP, every new team member learns a slightly different version, and clients get a different experience depending on who is working on that day.

  • New people learn the job faster
  • Every client gets the same experience, whoever is working that day
  • When something goes wrong, you can find the step that was missed and fix it
  • The owner, professional or executive can step away without everything stopping

02 · Work instructions

Done the same way, every time

A work instruction is a walkthrough for one task, one service or one product. It could be how a client intake is done, how a report or proposal is prepared, how a service is delivered from booking to follow-up, or how an order is packed and shipped. It makes sure the result is the same no matter who does it.

What this does for you

When the steps aren’t written down, quality depends on who happens to be working. When they’re written down, anyone trained can deliver the same result.

  • Clients get what they paid for
  • Your costs stay accurate because nobody does it their own way
  • Training is faster: people follow the guide instead of guessing
  • The work keeps going when someone is sick or leaves

03 · Training and decision maps

Teach people the system, guide their decisions

A system only works if people know how to use it. Training gets everyone started the same way. Decision maps help them handle the situations that don’t have one right answer.

What this does for you

Not every situation is black and white, and a decision map doesn’t pretend it is. It gives everyone the same starting point: the questions to ask, the options to offer, and when to call someone else. People still use their judgment, but they’re guided.

  • The team can handle most situations without waiting for the owner, manager or professional in charge
  • Clients and customers get fair, consistent answers
  • Only the hard cases get escalated
  • New hires become useful faster

04 · Cost tracking

Know what everything costs

Whether you sell a product, a service, a project or your hours, you need to know what it costs you to deliver it. Without that, you can’t tell if your prices, rates or packages work or how much you keep.

What this does for you

Most businesses and professionals know what they charge. Fewer know what it costs them. The gap is usually in the small things people forget: software subscriptions, payment fees, travel, revisions you don’t bill for, admin hours, packaging, electricity, the minutes someone spends fixing a mistake. Each one feels too small to count, but altogether they decide whether you’re actually making money.

  • You can set prices, rates and packages with confidence instead of guessing
  • You see right away when a supplier raises prices
  • You know which products, services or clients make money
  • You can grow without losing margin along the way

The costs people forget

The small costs add up

A lot of owners only count the food on the plate. But every order also uses gas, electricity, water, a takeout box, a sauce cup, a napkin, a bit of dish soap. People think these are too small to matter. They’re not. One sauce cup is cheap, but a thousand a month is not, and if it’s not in the costing, it comes out of your profit.

  • Utilities: LPG gas, electricity for chillers, freezers, fans and lights, and water for cooking and dishwashing
  • Kitchen: frying oil and how often it’s changed, ice, garnish, and staff meals
  • Takeout: boxes, bags, sauce cups, cutlery, straws, napkins and wax liners
  • Cleaning: dish soap, sanitizer, test strips, trash bags, gloves and hairnets
  • Front of house: tissue, hand soap and paper towels for the restroom, and POS paper rolls
  • Payments: card and e-wallet fees, and delivery app commissions
  • Overhead: rent, permits and licenses, insurance and bookkeeping
  • Upkeep: repairs, pest control, broken plates and glasses
  • Loss: waste, spoiled stock and remade orders

I track every one of these, down to the smallest item, and add them as a cost per plate or per order. That way the menu price covers what it really costs to serve.

06 · Issue and loss tracking

Track every problem and what it costs

Every business, service and practice has things that go wrong: returns, refunds, complaints, damaged deliveries, warranty repairs, redone work, missed deadlines, no-shows, chargebacks. Each one costs money or time. If nobody tracks them, you don’t know how much you’re losing or why.

What this does for you

Without tracking, problems get handled one by one and then forgotten. With a tracker, they become information. You know where you stand, how much you’re losing each month, and what’s causing it. Then you can fix the cause instead of paying for the same mistake again.

It works for claims, returns, refunds, complaints, client disputes, redone work, no-shows, late deliveries or missed deadlines. For claims, it answers questions like:

  • Which products get the most defect claims, and whether to ask the vendor for a quality check or stop selling them
  • Which vendors cause the most defects, and how much credit they owe you
  • Which carrier or delivery partner damages the most items, and when to file claims, change packaging or switch carriers
  • Which routes, warehouses or delivery types see the most damage
  • Which listings lead to “not as described” claims, and which photos, colors or measurements to fix
  • Which fixes protect the most margin: touch-up kits, parts, repairs, discounts or replacements
  • Which months bring more claims, to plan staff and stock parts ahead
  • Which customers file repeated claims, to spot patterns early
  • What problems really cost as a share of revenue, and whether it’s going up or down

Sample query: damaged in transit, by carrier 
-- damaged in transit, last 30 days, by carrier
-- cost = refund + parts + labor + reshipping, minus what the carrier paid back
SELECT
    carrier,
    COUNT(*) AS claims,
    SUM(CASE WHEN resolution = 'repair' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS fixed_by_repair,
    ROUND(SUM(refund + parts + labor + reship - carrier_payout), 2) AS net_loss
FROM claims
WHERE claim_type = 'damaged in transit'
  AND opened_on >= DATE('now', '-30 days')
GROUP BY carrier
ORDER BY net_loss DESC;

Claims decision map

The guide I built to help everyone on the team make the same call
Rebuilt from my process
Every claim starts here

Intake

  1. Thank the customer and open the claim the same day.
  2. Ask for photos of the whole item, the damage up close, the box and the label.
  3. Pull the order, the listing, the vendor specs and the delivery record.
  4. Check timing: was it reported within the claim window, and was damage noted on delivery?
  5. Pick the claim type below. If it’s unclear, compare the listing against the photos first.

Damaged in transit Most common

Reach: logistics and carrier
  1. File the claim with the carrier right away, with photos and the delivery record.
  2. Cosmetic scratch or scuff: send a touch-up kit.
  3. Broken part like a leg or bracket: order the part from the vendor and send it, or book a local tasker to fit it.
  4. Get a tasker quote first: parts + labor + travel.
  5. Replace only if it’s structural, unsafe, or the repair costs more than about 40% of the product cost.
  6. If replacing, schedule pickup of the damaged item and send it to outlet or parts.

Repair first. The carrier claim pays back what it can.

Defective

Reach: the vendor
  1. Send the vendor photos and the order details, and ask them to confirm it’s a manufacturing defect.
  2. Offer the customer a fix: replacement parts or a tasker repair.
  3. If the customer is happy with the item otherwise, offer a discount to keep it.
  4. Ask the vendor for a credit or chargeback to recoup the cost of the fix.
  5. Log the SKU. Three defects on the same item goes on the watch list for a QC request.

The vendor made the mistake. The vendor should carry the cost.

Misrepresented, or buyer’s remorse?

Reach: the vendor, after checking the listing
  1. Compare the item to the listing: size, color, material, photos and description.
  2. If the listing was wrong: it’s misrepresented. Fix the listing that day, offer a discount to keep or a free return, and ask the vendor for credit if their specs were wrong.
  3. If the listing was right: it’s buyer’s remorse. Explain kindly, then follow the return policy.

Be fair either way. If we got it wrong, we own it. If we didn’t, we still help, within the policy.

Buyer’s remorse

Reach: customer, then logistics if returning
  1. Check the return window and condition rules.
  2. Offer a price to keep first, using the ladder below.
  3. If they still want to return, explain the return shipping and restocking fee before booking anything.
  4. Then follow the return steps.

A small discount usually costs less than a pickup.

When it has to come back

Returns, and getting the money back

  1. Get a pickup quote, including labor, and book it.
  2. Inspect the item when it arrives and photograph it.
  3. Refund minus return shipping and restocking fee, as the policy allows.
  4. Recoup the loss: send it back to the vendor if defective, file with the carrier if damaged in transit, or resell as open-box or outlet.
  5. Log the final net loss for the weekly report.

The rule behind the whole map: offer the cheapest fix that fully solves the customer’s problem, and always try to recoup the cost from whoever caused it.

Fixing what causes the claims

What I’d change to stop the same claim coming back

Solving a claim protects one order. Fixing what caused it protects every order after it. For each type of claim, these are the changes I’d put in place, and the number I’d watch to see if they worked.

Buyer’s remorse

Cause: The item matched the listing, but it wasn’t what the customer pictured.
  1. Add lifestyle photos in a real room, plus one photo with a person or common object for scale
  2. Show measurements in both inches and centimeters, including seat height, depth and box size
  3. Add a “Will it fit?” note: measure doorways, stairs and elevators before ordering
  4. Offer fabric and color swatches before a big purchase
  5. Add a disclaimer that colors can look different on screens
  6. Show the return policy and restocking fee clearly on the product page and at checkout, not only in the fine print
  7. Send a pre-delivery email with the dimensions and a photo, giving customers a chance to cancel before it ships
  8. Encourage reviews with customer photos

How I’d measure it: Remorse returns per SKU, before and after the listing changes

Damaged in transit

Cause: The product left fine and arrived broken.
  1. Set packaging standards per product type: corner protectors for tables, foam for legs, double-wall boxes
  2. Photograph every large item at dispatch as proof of condition for carrier claims
  3. Score each carrier monthly on damage rate and claim payouts, and move volume to the best one
  4. Use white glove delivery for large or fragile items where the numbers show it pays off
  5. Ask customers to inspect before signing and note any damage on the delivery receipt
  6. Check which routes or warehouses have the most damage, and fix handling there first
  7. Declare the right value, and carrier claims pay back the real cost

How I’d measure it: Damage rate per carrier and per product, and money recovered from carrier claims

Defective

Cause: The product was faulty before it left the vendor.
  1. Give vendors a quality checklist and ask for photos before each shipment
  2. Spot-check the first units of every new batch
  3. Keep a vendor scorecard: defect rate, response time, credits paid
  4. Put credit or chargeback terms for defects in the vendor agreement
  5. Pause a SKU after a set number of defects until the vendor fixes it
  6. Check if “defects” are really assembly problems, and improve the instructions if they are
  7. Stock common spare parts, and fixes go out the same week

How I’d measure it: Defect rate per vendor and per SKU, and vendor credits recovered

Misrepresented

Cause: The listing didn’t match what arrived.
  1. Check every new listing against the vendor’s spec sheet before it goes live
  2. Photograph a real sample in-house instead of using only vendor renders
  3. Use color-accurate photos in natural light
  4. Use one listing template for every product: dimensions, materials, finish, care and what’s included
  5. Flag any listing with two or more “not as described” claims for review that week
  6. Ask the vendor to confirm specs in writing. Then you can claim credit if they were wrong

How I’d measure it: “Not as described” claims per listing, before and after fixes

The same idea works for services and professionals. If clients keep asking for refunds or extra revisions, check whether the scope, timeline and price were clear before they signed. Then fix the proposal, the contract or the onboarding to stop it happening again.

My learning map

Built in Obsidian · started this year

This year I started mind mapping what I learn instead of just reading it. Every topic gets its own note, and I link it to the notes it relates to. The graph is what it looks like now. Each dot is a note and each line is a link I made.

A lot of my notes are about how the brain learns, and that’s what made me keep going. The brain has billions of neurons, and they talk to each other through connections called synapses. Every time you use a connection, it gets a little stronger. Neuroscientists say “neurons that fire together, wire together.” With practice and writing, the pathway even gets insulated, and signals travel faster. That’s neuroplasticity: the brain keeps changing based on what you do with it at any age.

When I link a new idea to something I already know, I’m giving it more than one path to come back. When I quiz myself instead of rereading, I’m using the pathway, not just looking at it. And when I come back to a note days later, the connection gets stronger again. My map looks the way it does because that’s how memory works too: everything connected to everything else.

This matters even more now with AI everywhere. It’s easy to let a tool give us the answer and skip the thinking. But the brain only gets stronger at what it actually does. If we stop working through problems ourselves, we lose the critical thinking, and we take away the fun from learning. I keep learning the slow way too: reading, connecting ideas, testing myself and writing essays. I don’t want to hand that part over to anything.

Every note has a short summary, questions to test myself and cited sources. It feels like training. The more I use it, the easier it gets to remember things.

I do the same thing for a business or a professional. I gather everything that’s all over the place, put it in order, and make it easy to find.

A sudoku solver

Python · a practice project

Automation is everywhere now. Zapier, Make, Google Apps Script and the AI tools built into most platforms let anyone set one up in an afternoon. The hard part comes later when it breaks, sends the wrong email or skips a row without telling anyone. Fixing that takes the logic and the basic code understanding behind it.

AI works the same way if you work with one. Every prompt and every automated run uses tokens and tokens cost money. When you understand how the tool reads a request, you can write tighter prompts, send it only the data it needs, and choose a plan that matches how much you actually use. You get the same results with a smaller bill. It’s lighter on the planet too. Every request runs through data centers that use real electricity and water, and fewer wasted runs means less of both.

I practice the logic behind both with small scripts like this one. It solves a sudoku on its own. It finds an empty square, tries the numbers 1 to 9, and backs up when a number breaks a rule. The automations I build for clients follow the same idea. They run on clear rules and small checks, and they know what to do when something doesn’t fit.

sudoku.py 
# sudoku.py
# fills in a sudoku by trying numbers and backing up when it gets stuck
# 0 means the square is empty

puzzle = [
    [5, 3, 0, 0, 7, 0, 0, 0, 0],
    [6, 0, 0, 1, 9, 5, 0, 0, 0],
    [0, 9, 8, 0, 0, 0, 0, 6, 0],
    [8, 0, 0, 0, 6, 0, 0, 0, 3],
    [4, 0, 0, 8, 0, 3, 0, 0, 1],
    [7, 0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0, 6],
    [0, 6, 0, 0, 0, 0, 2, 8, 0],
    [0, 0, 0, 4, 1, 9, 0, 0, 5],
    [0, 0, 0, 0, 8, 0, 0, 7, 9],
]


def next_empty(grid):
    for r in range(9):
        for c in range(9):
            if grid[r][c] == 0:
                return r, c
    return None


def fits(grid, r, c, n):
    if n in grid[r]:
        return False
    if n in [grid[i][c] for i in range(9)]:
        return False
    # top left corner of the 3x3 box this square is in
    br, bc = 3 * (r // 3), 3 * (c // 3)
    for i in range(br, br + 3):
        if n in grid[i][bc:bc + 3]:
            return False
    return True


def solve(grid):
    spot = next_empty(grid)
    if spot is None:
        return True  # nothing left to fill
    r, c = spot
    for n in range(1, 10):
        if fits(grid, r, c, n):
            grid[r][c] = n
            if solve(grid):
                return True
            grid[r][c] = 0  # dead end, undo and try the next number
    return False


if solve(puzzle):
    for row in puzzle:
        print(" ".join(str(n) for n in row))
else:
    print("no solution, double check the puzzle")

Why I work this way

I’ve always needed to see how things fit together. Without a system, there’s no clear process to follow, no learning path for new people, and no decision path when something goes wrong. Work gets spread across ten different places and time gets lost. People look for files, ask the same question twice, and fix the same mistake again. A system stops that. Everything has a place, every task has a next step, and my energy goes into doing the work well instead of keeping track of it.

It’s the same for a team. When the steps are written down, work moves without anyone chasing it. New people learn faster. People know which calls they can make and when to ask. And when the numbers are in one place, you can see what’s going on. I’ve seen this in banking, tech support, eCommerce and my own restaurant. Only the details change.

The system I build for you won’t be a copy of mine. It has to work for both of us. Before I set anything up, I’ll ask questions and learn how you like to work: how you want updates, when you’re at your best, what you never want to deal with, and what you always want to see first.
